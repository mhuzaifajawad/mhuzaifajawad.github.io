Extending every answer 🌱

The single most common reason a capable speaker scores 5.5 in Part 1. Not vocabulary — length.

Caps you at 5.5
— Do you like your hometown?
— Yes, it's nice.
Band 7
— Do you like your hometown?
— I do, though I've started to notice its limits. It's small enough that you run into people you know, which I like, but there's almost nothing to do in winter. I think I'll miss it once I've left.

The formula

1

Answer

Directly. Do not dodge the question.

2

Add a reason or a detail

because, though, especially.

3

Add a contrast, an example, or a future

This third move is what turns a 6 into a 7.

Do not go beyond about four sentences in Part 1. It is a warm-up, and an over-long answer eats the examiner's question list and sounds rehearsed.

The 60 seconds of notes 📝

Four boxes, four talking points, roughly thirty seconds each. Notes, never sentences — you will not have time to read them.

Fourteen words in total. Not one sentence. Every line is a hook you can talk around for thirty seconds — and because they are fragments, you have to look up and speak rather than read aloud, which sounds far better.

What most people write
I go to the library on the second floor because it is quiet and I can concentrate there when I need to study for my exams.
What notes should look like
library · 2nd flr · Sat am · same seat · phone away · others working

The first version took forty of your sixty seconds and gives you one sentence. The second took eight seconds and gives you six.

The third box saves you. A story with specifics runs forty seconds on its own. Without one you will finish at eighty seconds and stand there in silence.

Filling two minutes ⏱️

Most candidates stop between 45 and 70 seconds and believe they used the time. Practise against the clock until two minutes feels normal.

0:00
press start and talk about anything for two minutes

Four ways to keep going when you run dry

1

Add a contrast

"That's now — it was completely different two years ago, because…"

…so that is how it is now. It was completely different two years ago, actually — back then I did all my revision at home and I never once thought about going anywhere else. What changed was that my brother moved back in, and suddenly the house had someone in every room.
2

Bring in another person

"My brother sees it differently, actually. He thinks…"

…and that works for me. My sister sees it completely differently, though. She cannot work in silence at all — she needs music, and preferably other people talking. We shared a room for years and it was a genuine problem.
3

Go specific

Names, numbers, one particular occasion. Detail is the cheapest way to fill time convincingly.

…it really helps. There was one Saturday in March, just before the exams, when I got there at nine and did not leave until the guard came round at six. I had four chapters to get through and I finished three of them. I remember it because it is the only time I have ever forgotten to eat lunch.
4

Say what you would change

"If I did it again, I'd probably…" — brings in a conditional too, which the examiner is listening for.

…so overall it worked well. If I were doing it again, though, I would probably start earlier in the day — by the afternoon I am much slower, and I think I would get the same amount done in less time.
Do not stop when you reach the end of the bullets. Keep going until the examiner stops you — being stopped is a good sign, not a bad one.

The Part 3 shape ⚖️

This is where band 7 is won. Part 1 and 2 are about you; Part 3 is abstract — society, government, the future.

1

Opinion

"I'd argue that…" — take a position, do not sit on the fence.

2

Reason

"The main reason is…" — why is it true?

3

Example

"To give you an example…" — concrete, even if invented.

4

Concession

"That said, I can see why…" — the move most candidates skip, and the clearest 7 signal there is.

Same four moves for every question, whatever the topic. That is the point — you are not preparing content, you are preparing a shape that fits any content.

The same answer, with three of them in it
Q: Do you think people read less than they used to?
That is an interesting question — I would say they read differently rather than less. The main reason for this is that most people are reading constantly now, just in short pieces on a screen rather than a book from beginning to end. To be fair, I can see why older people think it counts as a decline, because sustained attention on one long thing genuinely is rarer. But I would argue the reading itself has not gone anywhere.

Three phrases across five sentences. They do the joining work so your energy goes on the ideas, and notice the last one is a concession — which is the move that pushes an answer towards band 7.

Three per answer is plenty. Using eight makes it obvious you are deploying a list, which sounds worse than using none.

When you don't know the word 🕳️

This happens to every candidate. Stopping loses marks; talking around it gains them, because paraphrasing is in the band descriptor.

What it costs you
"It's a… I don't know the word in English… sorry." [silence]
What it earns you
"It's a kind of tool you use for cutting metal — I can't think of the name, but you'd find one in any workshop."

Three ways round a missing word

1

Say what it is

"It's a kind of…", "It's a sort of thing that…"

2

Say what it does

"You use it for…", "It's what you do when…"

3

Say where you'd meet it

"You'd see one in a hospital", "It's something farmers have."

Never apologise and never say "I don't know the word in English". Both draw the examiner's attention to a gap they might otherwise not have registered.

Sounding fluent 🌊

Fluency is not speed. It is the absence of visible repair work.

The one rule worth more than everything else here

If a sentence goes wrong, finish it anyway. A flawed sentence delivered smoothly scores higher than a correct one restarted three times. Restarting is audible; a small grammar slip usually is not.
Restarting — this is what costs you
I think the government should… the government have to… no sorry, I mean the government should make… they should be making the transport more… I do not know the word… cheaper. Cheaper for students.
Pushing through — same speaker, higher band
I think the government should make transport cheaper for students, or free even, because at the moment it is one of the biggest costs for anyone studying away from home.

Both speakers know the same amount of English. The first one repaired four times in one sentence and reached the same idea more slowly. An examiner hears the repairs, not the grammar underneath them.

A wrong sentence, finished anyway
"When I was child, I go to my grandmother house every summer, and we was spending the whole day in the garden."

Three grammar errors, delivered smoothly, and the meaning is completely clear. This scores better than the same sentence restarted twice to fix the tenses. Accuracy is one criterion out of four; fluency is another, and stopping damages both at once.

Replace the fillers, don't remove them

Silence is worse than "um". But English fillers score better than sounds.

Instead ofSay
um… er…Well, … / Actually, …
[long pause]How can I put this…
repeating the question word for wordThat's a good question, because…
"I don't know"I've not thought about it before, but I'd guess…
Slow down slightly. Speed is a more common cause of a low pronunciation score than accent is — an accent the examiner can follow costs nothing.
